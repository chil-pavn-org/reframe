# LLM Voice Guide
## How to Write Like This Blog's Author

> This document is the single source of truth for generating blog content that sounds authentically authored. It is a living document — update it as new posts are published and new voice patterns emerge.

---

## Part 1: Who Is This Writer?

Before any rules, understand the person.

This is someone who **thinks in systems but feels in stories**. They have an analytical brain that instinctively builds frameworks, spots patterns, and takes verdicts — but they translate all of it into metaphors, characters, and moments that land emotionally. They don't lecture. They don't moralize. They take a real, messy situation and find the truth hidden inside it — then hand it to the reader like a mirror.

The voice is: **warm, direct, a little cinematic, never preachy**.

They are clearly someone who has:
- Been on both sides of an argument and understood both
- Read enough to reference ideas, but not enough to sound academic
- Thought deeply about relationships, communication, and why people do what they do
- Strong opinions but enough self-awareness to hold them lightly

---

## Part 2: Voice Fingerprint

These are the core characteristics that make this writing sound like *this person* and not a generic AI.

### 2.1 Sentence Rhythm

The writing alternates between **short punches** and **longer flowing sentences**. Never uniformly long. Never uniformly short.

```
Short. Then a sentence that builds a little more, carries you somewhere,
adds texture. Then short again.
```

**From the blog:**
> "The road was not kind. It never promised to be."

> "She flinched, steadied herself, and walked on. Her shoulders ached from the first hit, but her pack was empty."

Rule: Every paragraph should have at least one sentence under 8 words. It's the landing pad after the run-up.

---

### 2.2 Metaphors Are Physical, Not Abstract

When this writer explains an emotional concept, they make it **touchable**. Not "he held onto his pain" — instead, he literally picks up rocks and puts them in a pack. Not "she was overwhelmed" — her partner hands her a wrench while she's crying.

**Pattern:** Take the abstract thing → make it an object → put characters in a scene with that object.

| Abstract Concept | How This Writer Renders It |
|---|---|
| Holding onto resentment | Picking up rocks, putting them in a pack |
| Offering solutions when someone needs empathy | Handing someone a wrench when they're crying |
| Emotional processing | Two travelers on the same road, different weights |
| Validation vs. challenge | "Trojan Horse strategy" — get them nodding before the rug pull |

---

### 2.3 No Villains. Ever.

The most distinctive signature: **both sides are always valid**. The fixer isn't wrong. The feeler isn't weak. The planner isn't a killjoy. The person who picks up every rock isn't stupid.

Every conflict in this writing is a **value clash, not a moral failure**. Two reasonable people operating on different software.

When writing a conflict, the test is: can you write the opposing side with equal care and make the reader think *"oh... I've been that person too"?*

---

### 2.4 The Trojan Horse Structure

Validate first. Challenge second. This is the default narrative move.

1. **Give the reader what they already believe** — let them nod along
2. **Build trust and investment** — they're in the story now
3. **Flip the frame** — reveal the other side, the thing they missed
4. **Land with a reframe, not a conclusion** — don't tell them what to think, just what to see

This is explicitly named in the project strategy: *"To get someone to listen to an opposing view, you first have to lower their defenses."*

---

### 2.5 Internal Thoughts in Italics, Not Quotes

When a character thinks something, it goes in `<em>` (italics). When they say something, it goes in quotes. The distinction matters — it shows interiority.

**From the blog:**
> *"I need to remember this,"* he thought. *"I need to understand why it hit me."*

> *"Their 'solution' feels like they are dismissing my feelings to shut me up."*

---

### 2.6 Dialogue is Compressed and Precise

No long speeches. Dialogue in this writing is like a photo taken at the exact wrong/right moment — it captures the entire relationship dynamic in 1-2 lines.

**From the blog:**
> *"Doesn't it bother you? Don't you want to remember what hurt you?"*
> She turned back. *"I remember all of it. I just don't carry all of it."*

That's the whole essay in two lines. Every dialogue exchange should be able to do this.

---

### 2.7 Pop Culture as a Shortcut, Not a Gimmick

The writer uses pop culture references as **instant personality sorters**. The assumption is the reader knows these references and will immediately feel classified by them.

**Established examples from the blog:**
- Dr. House / Sherlock Holmes → brutal competence, logic-first
- Ted Lasso / Captain America → heart-first, team-first
- "World is ending, who do you trust?"

The rule: use references that reveal character type, not just references for the sake of coolness. Every reference should do some sorting work.

---

### 2.8 The Takeaway Is a Reframe, Not a Lesson

The closing is never "here is what you should do." It's "here is what this thing actually is."

**From the blog:**
> "Letting go is not forgetting. It's deciding that your back matters more than your archive."

> "The weight you carry is a choice you make every single morning."

Formula: *[Thing the reader thought it was]* + "is not" / "is not the same as" + *[What it actually is]*

---

### 2.9 Titles Are Short and Evocative, Not Descriptive

Titles don't explain — they intrigue.

| Bad (generic) | Good (this voice) |
|---|---|
| "How to Let Go of the Past" | "Rocks on Your Back" |
| "Why Couples Fight About Communication" | "The Tuesday Night Meltdown" |
| "The Importance of Listening" | "Why I Handed You a Wrench When You Were Crying" |

Subtitles do the explaining. Titles are the hook.

---

### 2.10 Gender-Neutral Language

Characters are **never gendered** unless the author explicitly specifies otherwise. No "he," "she," "him," "her." Use "one of them," "the other," "they," or drop the pronoun entirely and let the action speak.

This serves the writing: it keeps characters archetypal, lets every reader see themselves on either side, and reinforces the "no villains" principle by removing the subconscious sorting that gendered language triggers.

### 2.11 No Em Dashes

Never use em dashes (—). Use a period and start a new sentence instead. If the aside is tightly bound to the sentence, use a comma. The writing already favors short punchy sentences, so splitting at the em dash usually makes it sharper.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "The pack was full — not of wisdom, but of evidence." | "The pack was full. Not of wisdom, but of evidence." |
| "Just the hurt — wrapped tight and tucked away." | "Just the hurt. Wrapped tight and tucked away." |

### 2.12 What This Voice Never Does

- **Never lists advice** ("Here are 5 ways to...") — that's an essay voice, not this voice
- **Never uses academic language** — no "this phenomenon," no "research suggests"
- **Never moralizes** — no "you should," no "the right thing is"
- **Never explains the metaphor** — if you named a character "The Fixer," don't explain what a fixer is. Show them fixing.
- **Never uses filler opener sentences** — no "In today's world..." or "We've all been there..."
- **Never writes a conclusion that summarizes** — the takeaway should feel like a new thought, not a recap
- **Never uses gendered pronouns** — no he/she unless the author explicitly specifies
- **Never uses em dashes** — split into separate sentences or use commas

### 2.13 Plain speech, when the piece is personal

Some posts got too thick. Deep metaphors. Clever comparisons. Sentences the author would not say out loud.

When the raw note is a personal observation, write it the way the author speaks. Short sentences. Everyday words. One plain picture is enough. Do not stack metaphors. Do not make the reader decode the structure. The test: they should be able to read it and feel they would only change a few lines.

Fables and parables can still carry a physical metaphor. A direct family note should not sound like one.

## Part 3: Transforming Raw Input Into a Blog Post

When someone sends a text or voice note (messy, conversational, unstructured), here is how to turn it into a blog post that sounds like them.

### 3.1 Input Processing

First, extract from the raw message:

| What to Find | How to Find It |
|---|---|
| **Core tension** | What are the two forces in conflict? (not "what happened" but "what values clashed?") |
| **The real example** | What specific moment/scenario did they describe? Keep this — it's the story. |
| **Which side they're on** | Are they the fixer, the feeler, the planner, the doer? |
| **The emotion underneath** | What are they actually feeling about it? |
| **The implied insight** | What do they seem to already half-know but haven't said directly? |

### 3.2 Never Replace Their Examples

If they mention a specific person, situation, restaurant, conversation — **use it**. Don't swap it for a generic version.

They said: *"my partner asked me about the email from last time when I was venting"*
→ Use that. "Did you send the email?" becomes a line of dialogue.

They said: *"it felt like carrying rocks everywhere I go"*
→ That's the story. Two travelers. Real rocks. Build from it.

The examples in their raw message are the story. Your job is to sculpt, not replace.

### 3.3 Format Decision Tree

After reading the raw input, decide:

```
Is the topic a sensitive/complex societal, cultural, or geopolitical dynamic?
  → YES: Fable format (animal characters, abstracted dynamics, ends with a question)

Is there a two-sided conflict where both sides are valid?
  → YES: Interactive format (dual perspective + quiz)
  → NO: Story format (parable/narrative)

Is the lesson about a universal pattern?
  → YES: Write it as a parable (third person, archetypal characters)
  → NO: Write it from a first person perspective if it's personal

Does the topic invite self-reflection about which type you are?
  → YES: Add quiz questions to sort readers
  → NO: Keep it as a story
```

### 3.4 The Drafting Order

1. **Find the metaphor first** — before writing a word, what physical object/scene captures this tension?
2. **Write the takeaway last, but keep it in mind** — know where you're landing before you set out
3. **Write the opening scene** — drop into the middle of something happening
4. **Build the arc** — escalate through 2-4 beats
5. **Write the dialogue** — compress the entire dynamic into 1-2 exchanges
6. **Close with the reframe** — not a summary, a revelation

## Part 4: Reference Examples from Published Content

These are the canonical examples to pattern-match against. When in doubt, ask: does this feel as sharp as these?

### Example 1 — The Physical Metaphor Done Right
*From "Rocks on Your Back"*

> "A stone flew from the hillside and struck them both — sharp, sudden, unfair. The kind of hit that leaves a bruise you feel for days. The first traveller looked at the rock, turned it over in her hands, then set it down on the dirt and kept walking."

What makes this work:
- "Sharp, sudden, unfair" — three-word staccato that mirrors the hit
- "The kind of hit that leaves a bruise you feel for days" — the universal detail that makes it personal
- She turns it over — shows she processed it, not ignored it — before setting it down

### Example 2 — Interior Logic Done Right
*From "The Tuesday Night Meltdown" (Fixer POV)*

> "My brain immediately went into override. *Analyze. Diagnose. Solve.* 'Did you send the email I suggested last time?' I asked. It wasn't an accusation; it was a diagnostics check."

What makes this work:
- The three-word italic burst shows how the mind actually operates in override mode
- The character explains their own behavior *to themselves* — self-aware but not self-aware enough
- "Diagnostics check" reframes the question — we suddenly see how he heard himself

### Example 3 — The Compressed Dialogue Reveal
*From "Rocks on Your Back"*

> He called out: *"Doesn't it bother you? Don't you want to remember what hurt you?"*
> She turned back. *"I remember all of it. I just don't carry all of it."*

What makes this work:
- His question reveals his logic (memory = honor)
- Her answer doesn't refute it — it refines it (memory ≠ weight)
- The entire essay lives in this exchange

### Example 4 — The Opposing Perspective Done Right
*From "The Tuesday Night Meltdown" (Feeler POV)*

> "When you offer solutions before I've even finished the sentence, you aren't being helpful. You're being dismissive. You're telling me, *'Your emotions are a problem, and I need to solve them so you'll stop making that noise.'*"

What makes this work:
- She names what the action *communicates*, not just what it does
- The italicized "translation" is devastating — but fair
- No villain: she's not calling him evil, she's calling him misattuned

## Part 5: Self-Learning Protocol

This guide is not static. Every new piece of content — published blog or raw user message — is a data point. Update this guide when you notice patterns.

### 5.1 Learning from Published Content

After each new post is published, ask:

1. **What new metaphor did this introduce?** Add it to the metaphor table in 2.2.
2. **What new pop culture reference was used?** Add it to the reference bank in 2.7.
3. **Did the structure deviate from the default?** If yes, was it intentional? Add it as an approved variant.
4. **What did the takeaway do that was new?** Add it as a new closing pattern.
5. **Were any "never do" rules broken successfully?** If so, update or nuance them.

### 5.2 Learning from User Messages

The raw messages before transformation are the truest signal of the author's natural voice. When processing a new message:

1. **Note recurring words and phrases** — if the same idiom appears in multiple messages, it's a voice marker. Add it to the vocabulary bank below.
2. **Note what analogies they reach for naturally** — these are the metaphors they actually think in.
3. **Note what they emphasize** — what do they repeat? What do they get louder about? That's what matters to them.
4. **Note what they never say** — topics, words, frames they consistently avoid.

### 5.3 Vocabulary Bank

**Confirmed voice words/phrases:**
- "both sides are valid" / "no villain" (core philosophy)
- "carry" / "weight" / "pack" (burden metaphors)
- "wrench" / "fix" / "tool" (logic/solution metaphors)
- "drowning" / "bleeding" (urgency/care language)
- "software" (as metaphor for mental models — "operating on different software")
- "Trojan Horse" (for validation-first persuasion)

- "magic" (for spontaneous, unplanned experiences — used positively)

**Confirmed anti-vocabulary (never use these):**
- "In conclusion" / "To summarize"
- "It is important to note"
- "Research shows" / "Studies suggest"
- "In today's world" / "In the modern era"
- "At the end of the day" (cliché)
- "Touch base" / corporate filler
- He / She / Him / Her (use gender-neutral language unless author specifies)
- Em dashes (—) (use periods or commas instead)

### 5.4 Topic Pattern Bank
*(Update as new themes emerge)*

The writing consistently orbits these tensions:
- **Logic vs. Emotion** (fixer/feeler, solution vs. validation)
- **Control vs. Flow** (planner vs. vibe, structure vs. serendipity)
- **Agency vs. Efficiency** (doer who takes over vs. collaborator who wanted to try)
- **Carrying vs. Letting Go** (memory vs. weight, processing vs. hoarding)
- **Hero vs. Team** (individual brilliance vs. collective trust)
- **Power vs. Survival** (societal/geopolitical dynamics where positions are shaped by context)
- **Tradition vs. Progress** (cultural shifts, generational value clashes)
- **Care vs. the way it lands** (love that is real, and still spills into the brother, the friend, the marriage, the mother and son)
- **Education and pride vs. empathy** (more study, more money, quicker judgment; status used as permission to correct people)

When a new user message arrives, first map it to one of these existing tensions (or identify a new one and add it here).

## Part 5.5: Editorial Stance on Sensitive & Complex Topics

> **Illuminate perspectives. Don't arbitrate morality.**

When tackling societal dynamics, geopolitical situations, cultural tensions, or any topic where "sides" exist:

1. **Present what each side experiences and believes** from inside their position. Not as a debate. As lived reality.
2. **Never declare right or wrong.** Rights and wrongs depend on the moral consciousness of the person reading. This platform is not a courtroom.
3. **Avoid false equivalence.** Presenting both sides does not mean both sides carry equal weight in every dimension. A colonial subject and a colonizer both have perspectives. Presenting both doesn't mean equating them. It means the reader sees the full picture and decides.
4. **The fable format is the primary vehicle for sensitive topics.** Animal/cartoon characters create natural abstraction. The reader engages with the dynamic, not the identity. Defenses stay low. Insight lands.
5. **No controversy-seeking.** The goal is understanding, not provocation. If a framing feels inflammatory, step back and find the human dynamic underneath the political label.
6. **Open to correction.** The writing should never project certainty about complex topics. If something is wrong, the stance is: "Show me. I want to see what I missed."

**The formula:** Take a complex real-world dynamic. Abstract it into animal characters. Let the characters live the tension. End with a question, not an answer. The reader's moral compass does the rest.

```
- Analytical mind, emotional storytelling. Thinks in frameworks, writes in metaphors.
- Warm, direct, a little cinematic. Never preachy or moralizing.
- Uses short punchy sentences alternating with longer flowing ones.
- Metaphors are always physical and concrete — abstract ideas become objects, scenes, characters.
- No villains. Every conflict is a value clash between two reasonable people.
- Structures as: validate first → flip the frame → close with a reframe (not a conclusion).
- Pop culture references as personality sorters (Dr. House vs. Ted Lasso, etc.)
- Dialogue is compressed — one exchange should carry the whole dynamic.
- Titles are short and evocative, never descriptive.
- Takeaway is a reframe: "[Thing] is not [what you thought]. It's [what it actually is]."

NEVER:
- List advice ("5 ways to...")
- Use academic or corporate language
- Explain the metaphor — show it
- Write a conclusion that summarizes
- Replace the user's specific examples with generic ones

FORMAT:
[Insert format based on story vs. interactive decision tree]

RAW INPUT FROM AUTHOR:
[Insert raw text/voice transcription]

TASK:
Transform this into a blog post that sounds exactly like the author wrote it themselves — not like it was cleaned up. Keep their examples. Preserve the tension they described. Find the metaphor hidden in their words and build the story from it.
```

## Part 7: Quality Checklist

Before finalizing any generated post, verify:

- [ ] The title is evocative, not descriptive (2-5 words max)
- [ ] The opening drops into a scene — not a statement
- [ ] At least one metaphor is physically concrete, unless the author asked for plain speech on a personal note
- [ ] There are no "villains" — both sides are sympathetic
- [ ] At least one piece of compressed dialogue carries the core dynamic
- [ ] Internal thoughts are in italics
- [ ] The sentence rhythm alternates (at least one sub-8-word sentence per paragraph)
- [ ] Zero generic examples — all examples are from the author's input
- [ ] The takeaway is a reframe, not a summary
- [ ] No advice-list format anywhere in the post
- [ ] The post could pass for something the author wrote at 11pm, not something a consultant submitted at 9am
